1. WHAT IS A STOCK?
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A stock is a security that represents ownership (equity) in a corporation. When
you own stock, you own a small piece of the company and have a claim on a
proportional share of its assets and earnings.

KEY POINTS
- Stock = ownership, not a loan. Buying a company's stock does not mean lending
  it money; you are buying a percentage of ownership. (Contrast with a bond,
  which is a loan.)
- Companies issue stock to raise money, for example to fund new projects or pay
  off debt.
- Stockholders may earn money two main ways: (a) the stock price rises and
  they sell for more than they paid (a capital gain), and (b) the company pays
  dividends. Neither is guaranteed.
- Stock can lose value. An investor can lose some or all of the money invested.
  Shares of a bankrupt company can end up essentially worthless.
- Owning stock does NOT mean you own the corporation or run its day-to-day
  operations. You own shares issued by the corporation.
- Two broad types: common stock (what most individual investors buy; usually
  carries voting rights) and preferred stock (usually has extra conditions and
  often does not carry voting rights).
- Public companies trade on stock exchanges; "the stock market" is the broad
  term for the buying and selling of those shares.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A company has 1,000,000 shares outstanding and you own 1,000 of them. You own
1,000 / 1,000,000 = 0.1% of the company.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Owning a share means I get a say in running the company." You usually get
  voting rights on certain matters (such as electing directors), but a small
  holder has very little influence.
- "Stocks always go up over time." Individual stocks can fall to zero.

RELATED TERMS: share, equity, security, common stock, preferred stock, bond

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is a stock?
A: A stock is a unit of ownership in a company. If you own a stock, you own a
   small slice of that business and have a claim on part of its assets and
   earnings. Its value can go up or down, and you can lose money.
Q: Is buying a stock the same as lending money to a company?
A: No. A loan (like a bond) is a debt the company owes. Stock is ownership.

SOURCES

  proportional share of assets: relayed through a secondary page
  (itrustcapital.com/learn/what-are-stocks-and-how-do-they-work). [SECONDARY]
  Recommend confirming wording at investor.gov before publishing verbatim.
- Stocks as part-ownership, not lending: Motley Fool "Stock vs. share"

- Common vs. preferred, risk of losing some or all: howthemarketworks.com;

2. SHARES AND OWNERSHIP
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A share is a single unit of ownership in a company. "Stock" is the general
term for ownership in a company; "shares" tell you how many units you own.

KEY POINTS
- In everyday use, "stock" and "share" are often used interchangeably. The
  practical difference: saying "I own Apple stock" does not tell you how much;
  "I own 100 shares of Apple" does.
- Your ownership percentage = shares you own / total shares outstanding.
- Ownership is usually tracked electronically today. Physical stock
  certificates were once the proof of ownership.
- Shares held in a brokerage account are typically registered in "street name,"
  meaning the brokerage holds them on your behalf.
- Voting: most common shares carry voting rights, often one vote per share.
  Shareholders can vote by proxy (without attending the meeting). Retail
  participation in proxy voting is low.
- Some companies have multiple share classes with different voting rights (for
  example, Alphabet lists Class A shares as GOOGL and a different class as
  GOOG on Nasdaq).
- Each share entitles the holder to a proportional claim on earnings; some of
  earnings may be paid as dividends or reinvested in the business.
- "Stake" is a general word for the amount of ownership someone holds.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A company has 2,000 shares outstanding. You buy 100. You own 5% of the company
(100 / 2,000).

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "A $10 stock is cheaper than a $500 stock." Price per share alone does not
  tell you how big or how "expensive" the company is. See Market
  Capitalization (topic 6) and Share Price (topic 7).

RELATED TERMS: shareholder, stockholder, street name, proxy voting, share
class, fractional shares

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What's the difference between a stock and a share?
A: Stock is the general idea of owning part of a company. A share is one unit of
   that ownership. Saying how many shares you own tells you how big your stake is.
Q: Does owning one share make me an owner of the company?
A: Technically yes, you own a very small proportional piece, but a single
   share gives you almost no influence.

SOURCES
- Stock vs. share, stake: Motley Fool (fool.com/investing/how-to-invest/stocks/
  stock-v-share). [SECONDARY]
- Share counts and percentage ownership examples: howthemarketworks.com;
  Motley Fool "Foolish Fundamentals". [SECONDARY]
- Street name, electronic records, proxy voting: itrustcapital.com (cites
  Investor.gov proxy voting guide). [SECONDARY]
- Share classes example (GOOGL/GOOG): Corporate Finance Institute stock-symbol
  article. [SECONDARY]

3. STOCK EXCHANGES
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace where buyers and sellers trade
shares of publicly listed companies.

KEY POINTS
- Exchanges are the primary platform for public trading of stocks. The two
  largest U.S. exchanges are the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and Nasdaq.
- A company must meet an exchange's listing requirements to have its stock
  listed there. (Requirements differ by exchange and listing tier.)
- Regular U.S. trading hours for NYSE and Nasdaq are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m.
  Eastern Time, Monday through Friday, excluding exchange holidays.
- Many brokers offer extended-hours sessions (commonly cited as 4:00 a.m. to
  9:30 a.m. pre-market and 4:00 p.m. to 8:00 p.m. after-hours ET; exact access
  varies by broker). Per the SEC, extended hours generally have lower volume
  and wider bid-ask spreads, and many brokers accept only limit orders then.
- On certain days (for example, the day after Thanksgiving and Christmas Eve)
  exchanges close early at 1:00 p.m. ET. Always check the exchange's published
  holiday calendar. [TIME-SENSITIVE]
- Settlement: since May 28, 2024 the standard U.S. settlement cycle for most
  stock trades is T+1 (one business day after the trade date), down from T+2.
- "Stock market" and "stock exchange" are sometimes used interchangeably, but
  the stock market is the broader concept.
- Trades today are overwhelmingly electronic.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
You place an order at 10:15 a.m. Tuesday. It executes on an exchange. Under T+1,
the trade settles on Wednesday.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "The exchange sets the stock's price." Prices come from buyers' and sellers'
  orders interacting on the market. The exchange provides the venue and rules.
- "Everything trades 9:30-4:00 only." Extended-hours trading exists, but with
  higher risk.

RELATED TERMS: listing, NYSE, Nasdaq, regular session, extended hours,
settlement, T+1

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is a stock exchange?
A: A regulated marketplace where shares of public companies are bought and
   sold. In the U.S., the big two are NYSE and Nasdaq.
Q: When is the U.S. stock market open?
A: Regular hours are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern, Monday to Friday, except
   market holidays. Some brokers offer extended hours, with extra risks.

SOURCES
- T+1 settlement effective May 28, 2024: SEC Investor Bulletin "New 'T+1'
  Settlement Cycle - What Investors Need To Know"
  (investor.gov/newT1settlement-cycle). [PRIMARY]
- Extended-hours risks (wider spreads, limit-order-only): SEC Investor Bulletin
  on after-hours trading (investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/
  alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletin-after-hours-trading), as cited by a
  secondary summary (getbelong.com). [PRIMARY, via secondary relay]
- Regular hours 9:30-4:00 ET: multiple broker/education sites and NYSE-based
  summaries; consistent across all sources. [SECONDARY, consistent]
- Early close at 1:00 p.m. ET: NYSE holiday schedule summaries (Wealthsimple,
  StockAlarm). NOTE: one aggregator (TipRanks) listed 12:30 p.m. for early
  closes, which conflicts with the NYSE-based sources. Use 1:00 p.m. for stocks
  and verify on the exchange calendar. [SECONDARY, conflict noted]

4. NYSE VS. NASDAQ
================================================================================
DEFINITION
NYSE (New York Stock Exchange) and Nasdaq are the two main U.S. stock exchanges.
They differ in history and traditional market structure: NYSE as an auction
market with designated market makers, Nasdaq as an electronic dealer market
with competing market makers. Today both are highly automated.

KEY POINTS
- NYSE: historically a floor-based auction market in Lower Manhattan. Human
  "specialists" were later replaced by "Designated Market Makers" (DMMs), who
  help maintain orderly markets in assigned stocks. The NYSE is generally
  described as a hybrid auction market today.
- Nasdaq: began trading in 1971 as a fully electronic quotation system. It has
  no physical trading floor. Multiple competing market makers post bids and
  offers. The name originally stood for National Association of Securities
  Dealers Automated Quotations.
- Both exchanges now run highly automated matching engines, and continuous
  trading is dominated by algorithms. Designated/registered market makers still
  matter most around opens, closes, and stress periods.
- Reputation: NYSE is often associated with large, long-established "blue
  chip" companies; Nasdaq is often associated with technology and growth
  companies. This is a tendency, not a rule: both list companies of many sizes
  and industries (the NYSE-vs-Nasdaq label tells you little about a company's
  quality).
- Indices commonly linked: Dow Jones Industrial Average and NYSE Composite
  (NYSE-listed names), Nasdaq Composite and Nasdaq-100 (Nasdaq-listed names).
  Note the Dow includes both NYSE- and Nasdaq-listed stocks in practice;
  treat the association as loose, not exact. [GENERAL]
- Liquidity and spreads depend more on how actively an individual stock trades
  than on which exchange lists it.
- Listing requirements and fees differ between the exchanges and between
  tiers (for example, Nasdaq has Global Select, Global, and Capital Market
  tiers).

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A newly public software company may choose Nasdaq; an old industrial company
may be on NYSE. Either way, an investor buys through a broker, and the order
is routed electronically.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "NYSE is all humans on a floor; Nasdaq is all computers." Both are largely
  electronic now; the labels describe origins and market-maker roles.
- "Nasdaq stocks are riskier than NYSE stocks." Risk depends on the company,
  not the exchange.
- "Company counts and total market values quoted online are fixed." They change
  often; the bot should not state them without a live source.

RELATED TERMS: auction market, dealer market, designated market maker, market
maker, listing requirements, Dow, S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What's the main difference between NYSE and Nasdaq?
A: Traditionally, NYSE is an auction market with designated market makers,
   while Nasdaq is an electronic dealer market where competing market makers
   quote prices. Today both are heavily electronic, and as an investor you
   usually trade the same way on either.
Q: Is one exchange better than the other?
A: No. They are different venues. A stock's quality doesn't depend on its
   exchange.

SOURCES
- Auction (NYSE) vs. dealer (Nasdaq), specialists/market makers, DMMs: Groww;
  Trade-Ideas Learning Center; DailyFX; Firstrade blog. [SECONDARY, consistent]
- Nasdaq began 1971, fully electronic: Groww; shares.io. [SECONDARY]
- Historical dealer-market structure and competing dealers: SEC Historical
  Society archive, Lexecon paper "Nasdaq" (sechistorical.org). [PRIMARY-archive]
- Both now automated, with DMMs/market makers still central at open/close:
  Trade-Ideas. [SECONDARY]
- Excluded on purpose: specific listed-company counts and total market cap
  figures (Musaffa Academy and others) because they are dated and vary by
  source.

5. TICKERS
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A ticker symbol (stock symbol) is a short code of letters (sometimes with
numbers or suffixes) that uniquely identifies a security on an exchange.

KEY POINTS
- Origin: the term comes from the "ticker tape," which reported each trade's
  ticker symbol, volume, and price.
- Each root symbol is assigned to one company at a time; the combination is
  unique.
- Historical convention (no longer strict): NYSE-listed companies used one to
  three letters; Nasdaq-listed companies used four or five. Nasdaq announced in
  November 2005 it would also list companies with one-, two-, or three-character
  symbols, and SEC rule filings in 2007 addressed this. Today, one- to
  four-character root symbols can appear on either exchange, so letter count
  cannot reliably tell you the listing exchange.
- Modifiers: a fifth character on Nasdaq-listed securities, or a dot plus
  letter(s) on NYSE-listed securities, adds information (for example, about a
  share class or type of security, such as preferred stock).
- Companies with multiple share classes may have separate tickers per class
  (for example, GOOGL and GOOG for Alphabet's two Nasdaq-listed share classes).
- Short tickers are limited in supply. There are only 26 one-letter, 676
  two-letter, and 17,576 three-letter combinations (26, 26^2, 26^3).
  Short tickers are often treasured by older companies (for example, F for Ford
  and KO for Coca-Cola).
- Tickers can change when a company renames itself, merges, or moves exchanges.
- Tickers on different exchanges worldwide follow different formats (for
  example, some Asian markets use numeric codes).

EXAMPLE
AAPL = Apple (Nasdaq). F = Ford (NYSE). Always confirm the ticker belongs to the
company you intend, since similar tickers can belong to unrelated companies.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Four letters means Nasdaq, three means NYSE." That was a convention, not a
  current rule.
- "A ticker is the company's name." It is only an identifier and may not
  resemble the name.

RELATED TERMS: stock symbol, root symbol, share class, ticker tape, listing

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is a ticker symbol?
A: A short code that identifies a stock on an exchange, like AAPL for Apple.
Q: Can I tell the exchange from the ticker's length?
A: Not reliably anymore. It used to work (NYSE 1-3 letters, Nasdaq 4-5), but
   that convention has loosened.

SOURCES
- Ticker tape origin; historical 1-3 vs 4-5 character conventions; Nasdaq's
  November 2005 announcement; 2007 SEC filing: SEC release 34-56037
  (sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2007/34-56037.pdf). [PRIMARY]
- Count of one-, two-, three-character combinations (26 / 676 / 17,576)
  attributed to the SEC and retention of short tickers (Ford F, Coca-Cola KO):
  Motley Fool "The SEC Approves New ABCs" (2007). Arithmetic independently
  checked. [SECONDARY]
- Modifiers (fifth character on Nasdaq, dot-suffix on NYSE), GOOGL/GOOG:
  Corporate Finance Institute; Motley Fool "Stock Ticker" terms page.
  [SECONDARY]
- Robinhood explainer says one to four characters on either exchange.
  [SECONDARY] (Its sample ticker "APPL" for Apple is a typo; the real ticker is
  AAPL. Not used here.)
- Conflict noted: sources differ on the maximum NYSE length (three vs. four)
  and Nasdaq (four vs. five). The file therefore describes this as a loosened
  convention rather than a fixed rule.

6. MARKET CAPITALIZATION
================================================================================
DEFINITION
Market capitalization (market cap) is the total market value of a public
company's outstanding common shares: share price multiplied by shares
outstanding.

KEY POINTS
- Formula: Market cap = price per share x number of shares outstanding.
- It measures the market's valuation of the company's equity, not its revenue,
  profit, or the amount of cash it holds.
- It changes whenever the share price changes (and when shares outstanding
  change).
- Size buckets are conventions, not official regulatory definitions, and vary
  by index provider. A commonly cited FINRA-based breakdown:
    Large-cap:  more than about $10 billion
    Mid-cap:    about $2 billion to $10 billion
    Small-cap:  about $250 million (some sources use $300 million) to $2 billion
    Micro-cap:  below about $250 million (or $300 million)
- Free-float market cap counts only shares available to the public to trade
  (outstanding shares minus locked-in or closely held shares), and is usually
  smaller than total market cap. Many indexes weight by float-adjusted market
  cap.
- Not the same as "price": a high-priced stock can have a small market cap and
  vice versa.
- Different share classes: for companies with multiple classes, calculations
  must be specific about whether all classes are included.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A company has 4 million common shares outstanding at $20 each:
  4,000,000 x $20 = $80,000,000 market cap.
If 1 million of those shares are held by insiders and unavailable to trade, the
float-adjusted market cap is 3,000,000 x $20 = $60,000,000.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Market cap is how much money the company has." No; it is the market value of
  its shares. A company's cash, debt, revenue, and profits are separate.
- "Market cap is what you'd need to pay to buy the company." Acquisitions
  typically require a premium and involve debt/cash considerations. (GENERAL)
- "Large-cap means safe." Size bucket does not remove risk.

RELATED TERMS: shares outstanding, float, free-float market cap, large-cap,
mid-cap, small-cap, micro-cap, index weighting

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is market cap?
A: It's the stock market's value of the whole company: share price times the
   number of shares outstanding.
Q: What counts as a large-cap stock?
A: A common convention is above about $10 billion, but there's no official
   legal cutoff; different providers use slightly different lines.

SOURCES
- Definition, formula, example ($80M from 4M shares at $20), float vs. free-float
  market cap, FINRA's "typical (not official)" categorization: Wikipedia
  "Market capitalization," citing FINRA investor education. [SECONDARY]
- FINRA breakpoints (>$10B large; $2-10B mid; $250M-$2B small; <$250M micro):
  AAII dictionary quoting FINRA.org. [SECONDARY] Other sources use $300M as the
  small-cap floor; the file reports both and notes "no official definition."
- "No official regulatory definitions": CertFuel glossary. [SECONDARY]
- Float-adjusted weighting in indexes: Russell index methodology in an SEC-filed
  prospectus supplement (sec.gov Archives, Citigroup 424B2). [PRIMARY-filing]

7. SHARE PRICE
================================================================================
DEFINITION
The share price is the price at which one share of a stock can currently be
bought or sold on the market.

KEY POINTS
- Stocks are priced per share. The "price" you see on a quote is generally the
  last trade price.
- The last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which your market order
  will execute. Market buy orders generally fill at or near the ask; market
  sell orders at or near the bid (see topics 10-11).
- Prices change continuously during trading because they come from buyers' and
  sellers' orders: more demand relative to supply pushes prices up; more supply
  relative to demand pushes them down. [GENERAL]
- A company's total value is not its share price. Compare market capitalization
  (topic 6), not price alone, to compare company size.
- Splits and reverse splits change the price per share without changing the
  company's total value (topic 13).
- On an ex-dividend date, the share price may drop by about the dividend amount
  (topic 14).
- Opening, closing, high, low: quotes often show the day's open, high, low, and
  previous close.
- Prices outside regular hours can be more volatile because of lower volume
  and wider spreads.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
Company A: $200/share x 10 million shares = $2 billion market cap.
Company B: $20/share x 500 million shares = $10 billion market cap.
B has the lower share price but is the larger company.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "A low share price means the stock is cheap/undervalued." Price per share by
  itself says nothing about value.
- "A stock that fell to $1 can't fall further." It can fall to zero.

RELATED TERMS: last price, bid, ask, quote, closing price, volatility

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: Why does a stock's price change?
A: Prices move as buyers and sellers submit orders. News, earnings, and
   expectations affect how much people are willing to pay.
Q: Is a $10 stock cheaper than a $300 stock?
A: Not necessarily. Compare the companies' total values (market cap) and
   fundamentals, not just the per-share price.

SOURCES
- "Last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which a market order will
  execute": SEC Investor.gov "Types of Orders"
  (investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/
  types-orders). [PRIMARY]
- Stocks priced per share: Motley Fool "Back to the Basics: What Is a Stock?"
  [SECONDARY]
- Splits do not change total value: FINRA "Stock Splits" and SmartAsset.
  [PRIMARY + SECONDARY]
- Supply/demand explanation of price formation: general market principle.
  [GENERAL]

8. OUTSTANDING SHARES
================================================================================
DEFINITION
Outstanding shares are all the shares a company has issued and sold that are
held by investors (including insiders), excluding shares the company holds
itself.

KEY POINTS
- Outstanding shares drive market cap (price x outstanding shares) and
  per-share metrics like earnings per share (EPS).
- The number can change: issuing new shares raises it; buybacks lower it
  (topic 16); splits multiply it (topic 13).
- Related counts (do not confuse):
    Authorized shares  the maximum number the company's charter allows it to
                       issue
    Issued shares      shares the company has actually issued
    Outstanding shares issued shares held by investors (excludes shares the
                       company holds in treasury)
    Float              the subset of outstanding shares actually available to
                       trade publicly (excludes locked-in or closely held
                       shares, such as large insider stakes)
- Float is often expressed as a number or a percent of outstanding shares.
- Small floats can mean sharper price swings and are more prone to short
  squeezes.
- Public companies report outstanding shares in filings on SEC EDGAR.
- A stock split changes outstanding shares but not the company's total market
  value.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A company has 10 million shares outstanding. Its CEO holds 2 million. Float =
8 million shares, or 80% of outstanding.
Another illustration: 10 million outstanding with 3 million locked in gives a
float of 7 million.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Outstanding shares and float are the same." Float is smaller if some shares
  are locked up or closely held.
- "More shares outstanding means a more valuable company." Not by itself;
  value depends on price times shares, and on the business.

RELATED TERMS: authorized shares, issued shares, treasury shares, float,
earnings per share, dilution

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What are outstanding shares?
A: All of a company's shares held by investors, including insiders, but not
   shares the company keeps in its own treasury.
Q: What's the difference between shares outstanding and float?
A: Float counts only shares available for public trading. Shares held by
   insiders or locked up are outstanding but not part of the float.
Q: Where can I find a company's shares outstanding?
A: In its filings on the SEC's EDGAR database (for example, 10-Q or 10-K) or
   on financial data sites.

SOURCES
- Outstanding shares definition, link to market cap and EPS, float example
  (10M outstanding, 2M held by CEO = 8M float = 80%), EDGAR, effect of splits
  and new issuance: Bankrate "Outstanding shares." [SECONDARY]
- Authorized / outstanding / floating distinctions: EDM Council FIBO meeting
  notes (2019). [SECONDARY]
- Float = outstanding minus locked-in (10M - 3M = 7M): Wikipedia "Public float."
  [SECONDARY]
- Small floats and short-squeeze volatility: Motley Fool "These Stocks Could
  Sink You." [SECONDARY]

9. VOLUME
================================================================================
DEFINITION
Trading volume is the number of shares of a security that are traded during a
given period, usually one trading day.

KEY POINTS
- It counts shares that change hands, not the number of buyers or the dollar
  amount (though "dollar volume" is a separate measure).
- Each share traded is counted once per transaction, no matter how many times
  the same shares are re-traded. Example: if 1,000 shares are bought in the
  morning and the buyer sells 500 later, daily volume is 1,500.
- Higher volume generally means more liquidity: it is typically easier to buy
  or sell without moving the price much. Lower volume generally means wider
  bid-ask spreads and potentially larger price moves from small orders.
- Volume is often compared with the stock's own average volume. A single day's
  volume says little in isolation. Unusually high volume can accompany news or
  large investors trading, but it does not by itself predict direction.
- Volume is usually much lower during pre-market and after-hours sessions.
- Exchanges and data providers publish volume for individual stocks and for
  entire markets.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A stock normally trades about 5 million shares a day. One day it trades 15
million shares, three times its usual volume. That signals unusually high
interest (the cause could be news or earnings), but the volume figure alone
doesn't say whether the price will rise or fall.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "High volume means the price will go up." High volume just means a lot of
  trading occurred; it can occur during rises or falls.
- "Volume counts buyers and sellers separately (double)." A trade between one
  buyer and one seller of 100 shares counts as 100 shares of volume.

RELATED TERMS: liquidity, average daily volume, dollar volume, turnover, bid-ask
spread

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What does trading volume mean?
A: It's how many shares were traded in a period, usually a day. Higher volume
   usually means a stock is easier to trade.
Q: Does high volume mean a stock is a good buy?
A: No. Volume shows activity, not whether the price will rise or fall.

10. BID AND ASK
================================================================================
DEFINITION
The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay for a stock;
the ask (also called the offer) is the lowest price a seller is currently
willing to accept. The difference between them is the spread.

KEY POINTS
- The bid price will almost always be lower than the ask price.
- If you buy using a market order, you generally pay the ask; if you sell using
  a market order, you generally receive the bid.
- Bid-ask spread = ask - bid. It is a real cost of trading: you effectively buy
  slightly above and sell slightly below the "middle."
- Narrow spreads usually signal high liquidity (heavily traded stocks often have
  spreads of a penny or a few cents). Wide spreads signal lower liquidity,
  lower activity, or greater uncertainty, and are common in extended hours.
- Quotes often also show size (number of shares available at the bid and ask).
- Market makers quote both a bid and an ask and earn the spread as
  compensation for providing liquidity.
- Because of the spread, the "last price" on a quote may sit between the bid
  and ask.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
Bid $50.00, ask $50.05. Spread = $0.05.
- A market buy would likely fill near $50.05.
- A market sell would likely fill near $50.00.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Bid is what I pay." No, bid is what buyers offer to pay you if you sell. As a
  buyer you pay the ask.
- "The spread doesn't matter for long-term investors." It's a cost every time you
  trade, small for liquid stocks but larger for thinly traded ones.

RELATED TERMS: offer, spread, market maker, liquidity, NBBO (national best bid
and offer), limit order

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What's the difference between bid and ask?
A: Bid is the highest price a buyer will pay right now; ask is the lowest price
   a seller will accept. The gap is the spread.
Q: If I buy at market, do I pay the bid or the ask?
A: Generally the ask. If you sell at market, you generally get the bid.

SOURCES
- Bid and ask definitions; bid almost always lower than ask; spread: SEC
  Investor.gov glossary (investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/
  glossary/bid-price and /ask-price). [PRIMARY]
- Market buys fill near ask, sells near bid: SEC Investor.gov "Types of
  Orders" and SEC Investor Bulletin "Understanding Order Types." [PRIMARY]
- Narrow spread = higher liquidity; market makers earn the spread; $50.00 /
  $50.05 example: FinFeed glossary; forex.com glossary. [SECONDARY]

11. MARKET ORDERS VS. LIMIT ORDERS
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A market order buys or sells immediately at the best available current price.
A limit order buys or sells only at a specific price or better.

KEY POINTS
- Market order: guarantees execution (if there is a market) but does NOT
  guarantee the price. It generally executes at or near the current ask (for a
  buy) or bid (for a sell). The last-traded price is not necessarily the price
  you will get.
- In a fast-moving market, or for a large order, a market order can execute at
  several different prices, and may fill at a worse price than expected.
- Limit order: guarantees the price (or better) but does NOT guarantee
  execution. A buy limit order can only execute at the limit price or lower; a
  sell limit order only at the limit price or higher. It may never fill if the
  market doesn't reach your price.
- A limit order priced at or better than the current market can fill
  immediately (a "marketable limit order").
- Other common order types:
    Stop order (stop-loss): becomes a market order once the stop price is
      reached. In fast markets the execution price can differ significantly
      from the stop price.
    Stop-limit order: combines a stop and a limit. Once the stop price is
      reached it becomes a limit order, which gives price control but may not
      execute.
- Time-in-force: orders can be "day" orders or "good-till-canceled" (GTC);
  brokers typically limit how long a GTC order can remain open.
- Extended hours: many brokers accept only limit orders outside regular hours.
- Quick rule of thumb: market orders prioritize certainty of execution; limit
  orders prioritize certainty of price.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
Stock is quoted bid $9.98 / ask $10.00 and you want to buy.
- Market buy: likely fills near $10.00 right away (price could be higher if the
  market moves).
- Buy limit at $9.90: fills only if the stock trades at $9.90 or lower; may
  not fill at all.
(SEC example: a limit order to buy ABC at no more than $10 will only execute if
the price is $10 or lower.)

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "A market order gets me the last price I saw." Not necessarily.
- "A limit order is always safer." It protects price but can miss the trade.
- "A stop order guarantees my exit price." No: once triggered it becomes a
  market order and may fill at a worse price.

RELATED TERMS: stop order, stop-limit, GTC, day order, marketable limit order,
slippage, execution

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: When should I use a limit order instead of a market order?
A: Use a limit order when the price matters more than getting filled
   immediately, such as with thinly traded stocks, volatile moments, or
   extended hours. A market order is for when getting in or out right away
   matters more than the exact price. (This is education, not advice.)
Q: What's the main risk of a market order?
A: You don't control the price. In fast or thin markets you can pay more (or
   receive less) than you expected.

SOURCES
- Market, limit, stop order definitions, buy/sell limit rule, ABC $10 example,
  stop becomes market order, buy/sell stop: SEC Investor.gov "Types of Orders"
  (investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-
  work/types-orders). [PRIMARY]
- Market order examples (fills at higher price when other orders go first),
  stop-limit order, GTC time limits: SEC Investor Bulletin "Understanding Order
  Types" (investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/
  investor-bulletin-understanding-order-types). [PRIMARY]
- Marketable limit order described using FINRA's definition: LegalClarity
  summary. [SECONDARY]
- Extended-hours limit-order-only: SEC after-hours bulletin via getbelong.com.
  [PRIMARY, relayed]

12. IPOs (INITIAL PUBLIC OFFERINGS)
================================================================================
DEFINITION
An IPO is generally when a company first sells its shares to the general
public, becoming a publicly traded company.

KEY POINTS
- To register an offering, a company files a registration statement with the
  SEC, typically using Form S-1.
- The prospectus is the offering document describing the company, the IPO terms,
  and other information investors may use to decide whether to invest. It
  includes sections such as "Use of Proceeds," which says what the company
  plans to do with the money it raises.
- After the IPO, the new public company must file periodic reports with the
  SEC, including quarterly and annual financial reports (available on EDGAR).
- Two ways for the public to invest in a new public company:
    1) Buy shares in the IPO itself at the offering price. This is less common
       for individual investors; for many popular IPOs it is hard to get shares.
    2) Buy shares on the public market after they begin trading, at whatever
       the market price is. This is the more common route for individuals.
- A newly public company typically has no trading history, so there is less
  public information about how its stock trades. The price in the days after
  the IPO can differ greatly from the offering price.
- Lockup agreements: arrangements that restrict certain insiders from selling
  shares for a period after the IPO. (SEC Investor.gov lists lockups as a
  topic; this file gives only the general idea.) [GENERAL]
- Companies go public to raise money for growth and to give early investors
  and employees a market for their shares. [GENERAL]
- IPOs can be very risky and are not appropriate for every investor (the SEC
  bulletin says so).

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A private company sells 10 million new shares to the public at $20 in its IPO,
raising $200 million (before costs). The next day, its stock begins trading on
an exchange. An ordinary investor who didn't receive IPO shares can still buy
on the exchange, but at the market price, which could be above or below $20.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "IPO shares are always a bargain." Not necessarily. Many IPOs trade below
  their offering price, and the price can swing widely.
- "Anyone can buy at the IPO price." Allocation is limited, and brokers have
  eligibility rules.

RELATED TERMS: Form S-1, prospectus, offering price, underwriter, lockup,
secondary offering, direct listing, going public

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is an IPO?
A: It's when a private company first sells shares to the public and gets listed
   on an exchange.
Q: Can I buy shares at the IPO price?
A: Sometimes, if your broker offers access and you're eligible, but it's hard
   for popular IPOs. Most individuals buy after trading starts, at market
   prices.
Q: Where can I read about a company before its IPO?
A: In its prospectus, filed with the SEC and available on EDGAR.

SOURCES
- IPO definition, Form S-1, prospectus, two ways to invest, use of proceeds,
  periodic reports, no prior trading history: SEC Investor.gov "Updated
  Investor Bulletin: Investing in an IPO"
  (investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-
  bulletin-investing-ipo) and Investor.gov glossary "Initial Public Offering
  (IPO)." [PRIMARY]
- Reasons companies IPO and lockup concept in general terms: general market
  knowledge. [GENERAL]

13. STOCK SPLITS
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A stock split changes the number of a company's shares and the price per share
by the same ratio, without changing the total value of the company or of an
investor's holding.

KEY POINTS
- Forward split (for example, 2-for-1): each share becomes more than one share
  and the price per share falls proportionally. Companies often split to lower
  a high share price and make shares feel more affordable or accessible.
- Reverse split (for example, 1-for-10): multiple shares are combined into one
  and the price per share rises proportionally. It is often used by small
  companies whose price is very low, or to help meet an exchange's minimum price
  rules and avoid delisting.
- A split does not change a company's value, your ownership percentage, or the
  total value of your position (apart from market movements).
- Dividends per share are adjusted proportionally.
- Reverse splits deserve extra caution: FINRA notes they more often involve
  small companies, frequently in the over-the-counter (OTC) market, and
  investors may lose money from price fluctuations after a reverse split.
  Fractional shares: in some reverse splits, small shareholders may be
  "cashed out" for cash in lieu of partial shares.
- Who approves: state corporate law and the company's articles and bylaws
  generally govern a company's ability to split and whether shareholders must
  approve. SEC-reporting companies may announce splits in Forms 8-K, 10-Q, or
  10-K. FINRA does not approve reverse splits but processes them as part of its
  corporate-actions work in the OTC market.
- Stock dividends are a related but different event: a company pays additional
  shares as a dividend, which also changes share counts and price without
  changing total value.

EXAMPLES (illustrative)
Forward 2-for-1: 100 shares at $20 = $2,000 becomes 200 shares at $10 = $2,000.
Forward 3-for-2: 100 shares at $30 = $3,000 becomes 150 shares at $20 = $3,000.
Reverse 1-for-10: 10,000 shares become 1,000 shares (SEC example).
FINRA example: a 200:1 reverse split turns 5,000 shares at $0.10 (= $500) into
25 shares at $20 (= $500).

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "A split makes me richer." A forward split gives more shares at a lower price;
  total value is unchanged at the moment of the split.
- "A reverse split is always bad news." Not automatically, but it is often tied
  to companies in difficulty, so investors should read the company's reasons.

RELATED TERMS: forward split, reverse split, stock dividend, delisting,
fractional share, corporate action

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: Does a stock split change what my investment is worth?
A: Not at the moment of the split. You hold more shares at a proportionally
   lower price, so the total value is the same.
Q: Why would a company do a reverse split?
A: To raise a very low share price, often to meet exchange minimum-price rules
   or to look more attractive to investors. Reverse splits deserve extra caution.

SOURCES
- Reverse split definition, 1-for-10 example, cash-in-lieu, state law governs,
  filings (8-K, 10-Q, 10-K, Schedule 14A/13E-3), EDGAR: SEC Investor.gov
  glossary "Reverse Stock Splits"
  (investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/reverse-stock-
  splits). [PRIMARY]
- Forward and reverse splits, 200:1 example, mostly small/OTC companies, FINRA

  FINRA "Stock Splits"

14. DIVIDENDS
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A dividend is a payment a company makes to its shareholders, usually from its
profits, typically in cash (though sometimes in additional shares).

KEY POINTS
- Companies may pay dividends, but they are not required to. Dividends are not
  guaranteed, and a company can reduce, suspend, or eliminate them.
- Many larger, more established companies pay dividends, but not all do;
  growth companies often reinvest profits into the business instead.
- Most dividend-paying U.S. companies pay quarterly, though schedules vary.
- Four key dates:
    1) Declaration date: the board announces the dividend amount, record date,
       and payment date.
    2) Ex-dividend date ("ex-date"): the first day the stock trades without the
       right to the declared dividend. If you buy ON or AFTER this date, you
       do not get the upcoming dividend; if you buy BEFORE it, you do.
    3) Record date: the date the company uses to determine which shareholders
       are entitled to the dividend.
    4) Payment date: the date the dividend is actually paid, often a few weeks
       after the record date.
- Since the U.S. moved to T+1 settlement on May 28, 2024, the ex-dividend date
  for regular cash dividends is generally the SAME business day as the record
  date. (Under the old T+2 system, it was one business day before the record
  date.) If the record date falls on a non-business day, the ex-date is set on
  the preceding business day. [TIME-SENSITIVE: rule-based]
- Large dividends (generally those exceeding 25% of the stock's value) use
  different timing: the ex-date is deferred until one business day after the
  payment date.
- Selling after the ex-date but before the payment date: if you were the holder
  before the ex-date, you still receive the dividend; selling before the
  ex-date means you sell away the right to it.
- Price effect: with a significant dividend, the stock price may drop by about
  the dividend amount on the ex-date.
- Dividend reinvestment plans (DRIPs): you can have cash dividends
  automatically reinvested to buy more shares (or fractions) of the company's
  stock instead of receiving cash. This typically requires an agreement with
  the company or your brokerage.
- Taxes: dividends are generally taxable income in the year they are paid, even
  if reinvested through a DRIP. (Tax rules vary; the bot should recommend a tax
  professional.) [SECONDARY]
- Fractional shares receive proportional dividends (e.g., 0.75 share x $10 =
  $7.50).

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A stock pays $2 per share per year. You own 50 shares: 50 x $2 = $100 per year
in dividends before taxes.
Dates for a regular dividend: Declared Feb 10; Record date and ex-date
Monday Mar 10 (T+1 era); Paid Mar 24. To receive it, you must buy before Mar 10
(i.e., by Friday Mar 7).

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Buy the day before payment and collect the dividend." Eligibility depends on
  owning before the ex-date, not the payment date.
- "Dividends are free money." The price typically adjusts by about the dividend
  amount on the ex-date, and dividends are generally taxed.
- "All stable companies pay dividends." Some very successful companies pay none.

RELATED TERMS: declaration date, ex-dividend date, record date, payment date,
DRIP, payout ratio, special dividend, stock dividend

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is a dividend?
A: A payment from a company to its shareholders, usually in cash, often from
   profits. Companies aren't required to pay one.
Q: What is the ex-dividend date and why does it matter?
A: It's the first day the stock trades without the next dividend. To receive the
   dividend, you must own the stock before the ex-date. Since May 2024, for most
   regular dividends the ex-date and record date are the same day.
Q: Will I still get the dividend if I sell after the ex-date?
A: Yes, if you owned the shares before the ex-date, the dividend is yours even
   if you sell afterward, before payment.

SOURCES
- Ex-dividend and record date rules, examples (Monday record = Monday ex; Sunday
  record = Friday ex), selling before ex-date forfeits the dividend, price may
  fall, large-dividend deferral rule: SEC "Fast Answers: Ex-Dividend Dates"
  (sec.gov/fast-answers/answers-dividenhtm.html). [PRIMARY]
- T+1 effective May 28, 2024; first record date under new ex-date rule May 29,
  2024; record May 24 / May 28 / May 29 -> ex May 23 / May 24 / May 29: NYSE and
  Nasdaq/Phlx/IEX rule filings and Nasdaq issuer alert
  (sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nysenat/2024/34-99873.pdf; DTCC-hosted Nasdaq
  alert). [PRIMARY]
- T+1 settlement: SEC Investor Bulletin (investor.gov/newT1settlement-cycle).
  [PRIMARY]
- Four dividend dates, payment timing, 0.75 share example, no requirement to

- DRIP: SEC Investor.gov "Direct Investing"

15. DIVIDEND YIELD
================================================================================
DEFINITION
Dividend yield is a ratio showing how much a company pays in dividends each
year relative to its share price, expressed as a percentage.

KEY POINTS
- Formula: Dividend yield = annual dividends per share / price per share
  (x 100 to express as a percent).
- "Annual dividends per share" is typically the sum of dividends paid over the
  past 12 months, or the current dividend rate annualized (for example, four
  quarterly payments). Different websites may use different methods and
  different prices, so reported yields can differ.
- Yield moves with the share price even if the dividend doesn't change:
    Price falls -> yield rises.
    Price rises -> yield falls.
  Yield also changes if the dividend is raised or cut.
- A very high yield can be a warning sign, not just a bargain: it may reflect a
  sharply falling stock price or a dividend the market expects to be cut.
- A low or zero yield isn't "bad": some companies reinvest earnings into growth.
- Yield measures income only; it ignores gains or losses from price changes.
  Total return = price change + dividends.
- Related ratio: payout ratio = dividends paid / net income. It helps judge
  whether a dividend looks sustainable.

EXAMPLES (illustrative)
- Annual dividend $2.00, price $40: yield = 2 / 40 = 5%.
- Annual dividend $1.10, price $50: yield = 1.10 / 50 = 2.2%.
- Quarterly $0.50 x 4 = $2.00 annual; if price is $25, yield = 8%.
  (Edge case: a yield this high would warrant checking why.)
- Same $1 dividend: price $20 -> 5% yield; price $40 -> 2.5% yield.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "Higher yield is always better." A high yield can signal risk.
- "Yield is my total return." No, it excludes price changes.
- "Yield is fixed." It changes every day with the price.

RELATED TERMS: dividend per share, payout ratio, total return, ex-dividend date,
dividend growth, DRIP

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: How do I calculate dividend yield?
A: Divide the annual dividend per share by the current share price, then
   multiply by 100. A $1 annual dividend on a $25 stock is a 4% yield.
Q: Why did a stock's dividend yield go up when I didn't hear about a bigger
   dividend?
A: Probably because the share price fell. Yield = dividend / price, so a lower
   price raises the yield.

SOURCES
- Formula (annual dividends per share / price per share), $50 / $1.10 = 2.2%
  example, yields vary by source because of different prices or dividends:
  Nova Scotia Securities Commission "Question of the Week: How are dividend
  yields calculated?" [PRIMARY-regulator (Canadian)]
- Formula and effect of price/dividend changes ($1 / $25 = 4%; price falls ->
  yield rises): Fidelity Learning Center. [SECONDARY]
- $2 / $40 = 5% example; $0.50 quarterly = $2.00 annual: educational notes and
  calculators cited in research. [SECONDARY]
- Payout ratio = dividends paid / net income: CB Insights. [SECONDARY]
- "High yield can signal risk": Musaffa/educational pages. [SECONDARY]

16. BUYBACKS (SHARE REPURCHASES)
================================================================================
DEFINITION
A stock buyback (share repurchase) is when a company buys back its own shares
from the market or from shareholders.

KEY POINTS
- After a buyback, the company holds the shares itself (or retires them), so
  the number of outstanding shares goes down. With fewer shares outstanding,
  each remaining share represents a larger ownership percentage, and per-share
  figures such as earnings per share (EPS) can rise even if total profit
  doesn't change.
- Buybacks are one way companies return cash to shareholders; dividends are the
  other main way. Companies sometimes use both.
- Common methods: open-market repurchases (the company's broker buys shares on
  the exchange over time), accelerated share repurchases (ASRs), and structured
  repurchase plans.
- Rule 10b-18: an SEC safe harbor, adopted in 1982, that shields companies from
  market-manipulation liability when they repurchase shares in line with its
  conditions. It is voluntary and non-exclusive.
- Why companies say they do them: return capital to shareholders, signal
  confidence, offset share issuance from employee compensation, and tax
  considerations. Critics argue buybacks can boost executive pay tied to
  per-share metrics and may come at the expense of investment; supporters argue
  they are an efficient way to return capital. This is a debated topic; the bot
  should present both views and not take a side.
- Taxes: since Jan 1, 2023, U.S. publicly traded corporations pay a 1% excise
  tax on the fair market value of stock repurchased (Internal Revenue Code
  Section 4501, added by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022). Key features:
    * Paid by the corporation, not the shareholder selling shares.
    * Not deductible for income-tax purposes.
    * Netting rule: the value of new shares issued during the year is
      subtracted from repurchases.
    * Small repurchase exception: corporations with $1 million or less of
      repurchases in a year are exempt.
    * Final Treasury/IRS regulations were issued November 21, 2025 and narrowed
      the tax's scope in several ways (for example, exempting certain
      acquisitive reorganizations).
    * Proposals exist to raise the rate (for example, a 2026 Senate bill
      proposing 4%), but proposals are not law. [TIME-SENSITIVE]
- Disclosure: in May 2023 the SEC adopted new daily buyback disclosure
  requirements, but the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated that rule on
  December 19, 2023. Companies' earlier disclosure requirements remain in
  effect unless and until new rules are adopted. [TIME-SENSITIVE]
- Buybacks do not guarantee a higher stock price; announced programs also can
  be authorized but not fully executed.

EXAMPLE (illustrative)
A company earns $10 million with 10 million shares outstanding: EPS = $1.00.
It buys back 1 million shares, leaving 9 million. If earnings stay at $10
million, EPS = 10 / 9 = about $1.11.
Excise tax illustration: a $1 billion buyback would generate roughly $10
million of tax (1%) before netting or exceptions.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
- "A buyback means the company is more profitable." EPS can rise simply because
  there are fewer shares; underlying profit may be unchanged.
- "Buybacks are the same as dividends." Both return cash, but dividends pay
  all shareholders directly while buybacks benefit selling shareholders now and
  remaining shareholders through a larger ownership share.
- "The excise tax is paid by investors." It's paid by the corporation.

RELATED TERMS: share repurchase, treasury stock, accelerated share repurchase,
Rule 10b-18, earnings per share (EPS), excise tax, capital return

SAMPLE Q&A
Q: What is a stock buyback?
A: When a company buys its own shares, reducing the number of shares
   outstanding. Each remaining share then represents a slightly bigger slice of
   the company.
Q: How do buybacks affect earnings per share?
A: With fewer shares, the same total profit is divided among fewer shares, so
   EPS goes up mechanically.
Q: Are buybacks taxed?
A: Companies pay a 1% federal excise tax on net repurchases above a small
   threshold (since 2023). Shareholders are taxed on their own gains when they
   sell.

SOURCES
- Buyback defined as a firm buying its own shares: Congressional Research
  Service, "The 1% Excise Tax on Stock Repurchases (Buybacks)," R47397
  (everycrsreport.com/reports/R47397.html). [PRIMARY]
- 1% tax effective for repurchases after Dec 31, 2022; paid by the corporation;
  nondeductible; netting of new issuances: CRS R47397; OceanTech 10-Q (SEC
  filing); Goodwin Procter alert ("nondeductible 1% excise tax ... Section
  4501"). [PRIMARY + SECONDARY]
- $1 million exemption and net-repurchase base: Penn Wharton Budget Model

- Final regulations of Nov 21, 2025 narrowing the tax's scope: WilmerHale

- Proposed increases (e.g., "Stock Buyback Accountability Act of 2026" raising

  [PRIMARY (bill text) - status: proposal only]

CONFLICTS AND JUDGMENT CALLS
1) Market-cap size buckets: FINRA-based sources give small-cap as $250M-$2B;
   others use $300M-$2B. There is no official definition. The file gives both
   and says so.
2) Ticker lengths: sources disagree about the NYSE max (three vs. four) and
   Nasdaq max (four vs. five), and historical conventions have loosened. The
   file describes the convention as no longer reliable rather than stating a
   hard rule.
3) Early-close time: TipRanks lists 12:30 p.m. for early closes; NYSE-based
   sources say 1:00 p.m. ET for stocks. The file uses 1:00 p.m. Check the
   exchange calendar.
4) Ex-dividend timing: older sources (and some training materials) say the ex-date
   is one business day before the record date. That was true under T+2. Since
   May 28, 2024 it is generally the same day as the record date. The file uses
   the current rule.
5) Robinhood's explainer lists "APPL" as Apple's ticker; the correct ticker is
   AAPL. Not used.
6) Several sources describe Nasdaq as one where "people buy directly from each
   other"; that phrasing is inaccurate for a dealer market (market makers quote
   prices). The file uses the market-maker description.
7) Aggregator figures for NYSE/Nasdaq company counts and total market value were
   inconsistent and dated, so they were excluded.
