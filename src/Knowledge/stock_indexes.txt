STOCK INDEXES: TERM DEFINITIONS

What is a stock market index?
A calculated measure of the performance of a group of stocks, used to represent a market, a segment of a market, or an industry. An index is a number, not something you can buy directly. Its value is computed from the prices of its component stocks using a specific rule set (which stocks are included and how each is weighted). Investors can gain exposure to an index through index funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) that hold its components. Index levels (for example, 5,000 points) are not dollar prices; changes are usually tracked as percentages.

S&P 500
Index of 500 large U.S. publicly traded companies, maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices. Widely used as the main benchmark for the U.S. large-cap stock market. Weighting: float-adjusted market capitalization (only shares available to public investors count). Companies are chosen by an index committee, not by a simple ranking, from those that meet eligibility criteria, which include a minimum market capitalization (a threshold S&P updates periodically; about $22.7 billion as of the July 2026 methodology), adequate trading liquidity and public float, U.S. domicile, and positive GAAP earnings. The eligibility rules apply to additions; an existing member that falls below the threshold is not automatically removed. Index level = sum of (price x float-adjusted shares) / index divisor.

Nasdaq Composite
Market-cap-weighted index that covers nearly all common stocks listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market, including some non-U.S. companies. Contains more than 3,000 securities (the number changes as companies list, merge, or delist). Excludes securities such as ETFs, preferred shares, warrants, and rights. Technology-heavy, with a small number of very large companies making up a large part of the total weight. Symbol: COMP.

Nasdaq-100
Index of the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market. Financial companies (including banks and insurers) are excluded. Weighting: modified market-cap weighting, which adjusts weights to limit the dominance of the largest companies. Rebalanced quarterly (March, June, September, December) and reconstituted annually in December, when membership is re-evaluated and companies are added or removed. It is a subset of the Nasdaq Composite universe, not a separate set of companies. Symbol: NDX.

Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA, "the Dow")
Index of 30 large, well-established U.S. companies, maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices. Components are selected by a committee, not by fixed quantitative rules, and companies are listed on the NYSE or Nasdaq. Weighting: price-weighted. Value = sum of the 30 share prices / the Dow divisor. The divisor is adjusted for stock splits, spin-offs, and component changes so the index stays continuous. A stock with a higher share price moves the index more than one with a lower share price, regardless of the company's actual size. With only 30 stocks, it is a narrower and less representative measure than the S&P 500. Not the same as the S&P 500 or the Nasdaq.

Russell 2000
Index of about 2,000 small-cap U.S. companies, maintained by FTSE Russell. Built by ranking the 3,000 largest U.S. companies by market cap (the Russell 3000); the largest 1,000 form the Russell 1000, and the remaining roughly 2,000 form the Russell 2000. Weighting: float-adjusted market cap. Rules-based membership (by market cap rank, not committee choice). Used as the main benchmark for U.S. small-cap stocks. Reconstitution: historically once a year in June; beginning in 2026, FTSE Russell moved to semi-annual reconstitution (June and December). Symbol: RUT.

Market-cap weighted indexes
Indexes where each company's weight is proportional to its market capitalization (share price x shares outstanding). Larger companies have a larger influence on the index's performance. Example: a company that is 5% of the total market cap of all components is 5% of the index. Weights shift automatically as prices change, so no trading is needed to maintain them. Some indexes (including the S&P 500) use float-adjusted market cap, which counts only publicly tradable shares. Downside: the index can become concentrated in a few very large companies. Examples: S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite, Russell 2000.

Price-weighted indexes
Indexes where each stock's influence is proportional to its share price, not its company size. Value = sum of component share prices / divisor. A $300 stock moves the index more than a $30 stock for the same percentage change. A stock split changes the share price and therefore the weight, which is why the divisor is adjusted. Example: Dow Jones Industrial Average.

Equal-weighted indexes
Indexes where every component gets the same weight regardless of size or price. Example: an equal-weighted index of 100 stocks gives each 1%. Because prices change, weights drift and must be reset periodically by rebalancing. Compared with market-cap weighting, equal weighting gives smaller companies more influence and reduces concentration in the largest companies, but typically has higher turnover and trading costs. Example: S&P 500 Equal Weight Index.

Index rebalancing
Periodic adjustment of an index's component weights back to the weights its rules call for. Frequency depends on the index (for example, the Nasdaq-100 is rebalanced quarterly). Distinct from reconstitution, which changes which companies are in the index (additions and deletions). Index funds that track the index must trade to match these changes, which can create trading volume and price movement around the effective date.

Why indexes are used as benchmarks
An index provides a standard yardstick to judge performance. Investors and fund managers compare a portfolio's or fund's return against a relevant index (for example, a U.S. large-cap stock fund against the S&P 500) to see whether it outperformed or underperformed the market segment it targets. The benchmark should match the investment's style and market segment. Indexes are also used to gauge overall market direction, as the basis for index funds and ETFs, and as a reference for measures such as beta and alpha. Indexes do not include fees or trading costs, so a real fund tracking one will return slightly less.


================================================================================
STOCK MARKET INDEXES
================================================================================

PURPOSE
This section explains major U.S. stock market indexes, how indexes are
constructed, how they differ, and how investors use them as benchmarks.

================================================================================
1. WHAT IS A STOCK MARKET INDEX?
================================================================================

DEFINITION

A stock market index is a measurement that tracks the performance of a selected
group of stocks.

Instead of following one company, an index combines information from many
companies into a single measurement. This makes it easier to understand how a
particular part of the stock market is performing.

KEY POINTS

- An index tracks a group of stocks.
- Different indexes track different groups of companies.
- Some indexes focus on large companies, while others focus on smaller companies
  or companies listed on a particular exchange.
- An index's weighting method determines how much influence each company has on
  the index.
- Index values are mainly useful for tracking changes over time.
- Investors often use indexes as benchmarks for comparing investment
  performance.
- An index itself is not an investment that can be purchased directly.

EXAMPLE

Suppose an index tracks a collection of large U.S. companies. If the combined
performance of those companies rises, the index generally rises. If their
combined performance falls, the index generally falls.

RELATED TERMS

benchmark, constituent, market capitalization, weighting, S&P 500, Dow Jones
Industrial Average, Nasdaq Composite, Russell 2000

================================================================================
2. S&P 500
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The S&P 500 is a stock market index designed to measure the performance of
approximately 500 large U.S. companies.

KEY POINTS

- The S&P 500 focuses primarily on large U.S. companies.
- It includes companies from many different sectors of the economy.
- It is commonly used as a benchmark for U.S. large-cap stocks.
- The S&P 500 uses float-adjusted market-capitalization weighting.
- Larger companies generally have more influence on the index than smaller
  companies.
- Having a high stock price alone does not give a company greater influence.
  The company's float-adjusted market capitalization is what matters for its
  weighting.
- The companies included in the S&P 500 can change over time.

EXAMPLE

Suppose Company A has a much larger float-adjusted market capitalization than
Company B. A similar percentage price movement in Company A generally has more
influence on the S&P 500 than the same percentage movement in Company B.

RELATED TERMS

large-cap, market capitalization, float-adjusted market capitalization,
benchmark, constituent, S&P Dow Jones Indices

================================================================================
3. DOW JONES INDUSTRIAL AVERAGE (DJIA)
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The Dow Jones Industrial Average, usually called the Dow or DJIA, is a U.S.
stock market index containing 30 prominent companies.

KEY POINTS

- The Dow contains far fewer companies than the S&P 500.
- It is often used as a general indicator of the performance of large,
  established U.S. companies.
- The Dow is price-weighted.
- This means stocks with higher share prices have more influence on the index
  than stocks with lower share prices.
- A company's influence in the Dow therefore does not directly correspond to
  the company's total market capitalization.
- The companies included in the Dow can change over time.

RELATED TERMS

DJIA, Dow, price-weighted index, blue-chip company, index constituent,
S&P 500

================================================================================
4. S&P 500 VS. DOW JONES INDUSTRIAL AVERAGE
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average are both widely followed U.S.
stock market indexes, but they track different collections of companies and
use different weighting methods.

KEY DIFFERENCES

NUMBER OF COMPANIES

S&P 500:
- Approximately 500 large U.S. companies.

Dow:
- 30 prominent U.S. companies.

WEIGHTING

S&P 500:
- Float-adjusted market-capitalization weighted.
- Larger companies generally have more influence.

Dow:
- Price weighted.
- Stocks with higher share prices have more influence.

MARKET COVERAGE

S&P 500:
- Includes a much larger collection of companies.
- Provides broader coverage of large U.S. companies.

Dow:
- Includes only 30 companies.
- Provides a narrower snapshot.

WHY THEY CAN MOVE DIFFERENTLY

The S&P 500 and Dow can move differently because:

1. They contain different collections of stocks.
2. They weight those stocks differently.
3. A high-priced stock can have substantial influence on the Dow because the
   Dow is price weighted.
4. Large-market-cap companies have greater influence on the S&P 500 because
   it is market-cap weighted.

SIMPLE EXPLANATION

The S&P 500 tracks roughly 500 large U.S. companies and gives greater weight
to companies with larger float-adjusted market values.

The Dow tracks only 30 companies and gives greater weight to stocks with
higher prices.

SAMPLE Q&A

Q: What's the difference between the S&P 500 and the Dow?

A: The biggest differences are how many companies they track and how they are
weighted. The S&P 500 contains roughly 500 large U.S. companies and is weighted
by float-adjusted market capitalization. The Dow contains 30 prominent companies
and is price weighted, meaning higher-priced stocks have more influence.
Because they contain different stocks and calculate their indexes differently,
they can perform differently.

Q: Is the S&P 500 just another name for the Dow?

A: No. They are separate indexes with different companies and different
weighting methods.

Q: Why can the Dow rise while the S&P 500 falls?

A: They contain different collections of companies and weight those companies
differently. The Dow's price-weighting system can also cause particular
high-priced stocks to have considerable influence on its movement.

RELATED TERMS

S&P 500, DJIA, market-cap weighting, price weighting, benchmark

================================================================================
5. NASDAQ COMPOSITE
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The Nasdaq Composite is an index that tracks a broad collection of securities
listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market.

KEY POINTS

- The Nasdaq Composite is associated with companies listed on Nasdaq.
- It includes substantially more companies than the Nasdaq-100.
- It is commonly associated with technology and growth companies because those
  companies make up an important portion of the Nasdaq market.
- The Nasdaq Composite and Nasdaq-100 are not the same index.
- The Nasdaq Composite is market-capitalization weighted.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"The Nasdaq and Nasdaq Composite are the same thing."

They are not. Nasdaq is a stock exchange, while the Nasdaq Composite is an
index tracking securities listed on that exchange.

RELATED TERMS

Nasdaq, Nasdaq-100, stock exchange, technology stocks, growth stocks

================================================================================
6. NASDAQ-100
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The Nasdaq-100 is an index consisting of 100 of the largest non-financial
companies listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market, subject to the index's eligibility
rules.

KEY POINTS

- Nasdaq-100 and Nasdaq Composite are different indexes.
- Nasdaq-100 is much more selective.
- Financial companies are excluded from the Nasdaq-100.
- The index is strongly associated with large technology and growth-oriented
  companies, although it is not exclusively a technology index.

NASDAQ COMPOSITE VS. NASDAQ-100

Nasdaq Composite:
- Broad collection of Nasdaq-listed securities.
- Covers a much larger portion of the Nasdaq market.

Nasdaq-100:
- Focuses on 100 large non-financial Nasdaq-listed companies.

RELATED TERMS

Nasdaq Composite, Nasdaq Stock Market, QQQ, technology, growth stocks

================================================================================
7. RUSSELL 2000
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The Russell 2000 is a U.S. stock market index designed to measure the
performance of approximately 2,000 smaller U.S. companies.

KEY POINTS

- The Russell 2000 is widely associated with small-cap stocks.
- It provides a different view of the market than indexes focused on large
  companies.
- It is commonly used as a benchmark for U.S. small-cap stock performance.
- Because it measures smaller companies, its performance can differ
  substantially from the S&P 500.

S&P 500 VS. RUSSELL 2000

S&P 500:
- Primarily large companies.

Russell 2000:
- Primarily smaller companies.

The indexes therefore measure different areas of the U.S. stock market.

RELATED TERMS

small-cap stocks, Russell indexes, benchmark, S&P 500, market capitalization

================================================================================
8. WILSHIRE 5000
================================================================================

DEFINITION

The Wilshire 5000 is a broad U.S. stock market index designed to represent a
large portion of the publicly traded U.S. equity market.

KEY POINTS

- It is intended to provide broad exposure to the U.S. equity market.
- It is broader than indexes such as the S&P 500 or Dow.
- Despite the name, the actual number of securities represented by an index can
  change over time.
- It can be used as a benchmark for examining broad U.S. equity-market
  performance.

RELATED TERMS

total market index, broad-market index, S&P 500, benchmark

================================================================================
9. MARKET-CAP WEIGHTING
================================================================================

DEFINITION

A market-capitalization-weighted index gives companies with larger market
capitalizations more influence over the index.

Market capitalization is generally:

Market Cap = Share Price x Shares Outstanding

KEY POINTS

- Larger companies receive larger weights.
- Smaller companies receive smaller weights.
- If a heavily weighted company moves substantially, it can have a greater
  effect on the index.
- The S&P 500 uses a float-adjusted version of market-cap weighting.

EXAMPLE

Imagine an index containing two companies:

Company A market value = $900 billion
Company B market value = $100 billion

Company A represents a much larger portion of the combined market value and
therefore has much more influence in a market-cap-weighted index.

RELATED TERMS

market capitalization, float-adjusted market cap, index weighting, S&P 500

================================================================================
10. PRICE WEIGHTING
================================================================================

DEFINITION

A price-weighted index gives stocks with higher share prices more influence
over the index.

KEY POINTS

- Weight depends heavily on the stock's share price.
- It does not directly depend on the company's total market capitalization.
- The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a well-known example.
- A higher-priced Dow stock can have more influence than a lower-priced stock
  even when the lower-priced company's total market capitalization is larger.

EXAMPLE

In a simplified price-weighted index:

Stock A = $200 per share
Stock B = $50 per share

Stock A would have more influence on the index because its share price is
higher.

RELATED TERMS

DJIA, Dow divisor, share price, market-cap weighting

================================================================================
11. EQUAL WEIGHTING
================================================================================

DEFINITION

An equal-weighted index assigns approximately the same weight to each company
in the index rather than giving larger or higher-priced companies more
influence.

EXAMPLE

If a simplified equal-weighted index contains 100 stocks, each would initially
represent approximately 1% of the index.

KEY POINTS

- Company size does not determine the initial weighting.
- Price movements cause weights to drift over time.
- Rebalancing is therefore needed to restore the target equal weights.
- Equal weighting produces different performance characteristics from
  market-cap weighting.

RELATED TERMS

weighting, rebalancing, market-cap weighting

================================================================================
12. INDEX CONSTITUENTS
================================================================================

DEFINITION

An index constituent is an individual security included in an index.

KEY POINTS

- Each index has rules determining which securities are eligible.
- Constituents can be added or removed.
- Different indexes use different eligibility and selection methods.
- A company can appear in more than one index.

EXAMPLE

If a company qualifies for both a large-cap index and another index based on
its listing or sector, the company's stock can be a constituent of multiple
indexes.

RELATED TERMS

index membership, component, eligibility, reconstitution

================================================================================
13. INDEX REBALANCING AND RECONSTITUTION
================================================================================

DEFINITION

Index maintenance includes periodically updating an index so that it continues
to follow its stated methodology.

REBALANCING

Rebalancing generally refers to adjusting the weights of securities within an
index.

RECONSTITUTION

Reconstitution generally refers to reviewing index membership and potentially
adding or removing securities.

KEY POINTS

- Indexes are not necessarily static.
- Companies can be added or removed.
- Weights can change.
- Different index providers use different schedules and methodologies.

RELATED TERMS

index constituent, weighting, methodology, index provider

================================================================================
14. WHAT DOES AN INDEX MEASURE?
================================================================================

An index measures the performance of the particular collection of securities
defined by its methodology.

Therefore, statements such as:

"The market increased today."

can be incomplete without identifying which market measurement is being used.

For example:

- S&P 500 represents a broad group of large U.S. companies.
- Dow represents a much smaller collection of prominent companies.
- Russell 2000 focuses on smaller U.S. companies.
- Nasdaq Composite focuses on securities listed on Nasdaq.

Different indexes can therefore move differently on the same day.

RELATED TERMS

market performance, benchmark, market segment

================================================================================
15. WHY ARE INDEXES USED AS BENCHMARKS?
================================================================================

DEFINITION

A benchmark provides a reference point that can be used to compare investment
performance.

EXAMPLE

If a portfolio returned 7% during a period while a relevant market index
returned 10%, comparing the two provides more information than looking at the
portfolio's 7% return alone.

However, a useful comparison requires an appropriate benchmark. Comparing a
portfolio of small companies with an index consisting primarily of large
companies may not provide an apples-to-apples comparison.

KEY POINTS

- Benchmarks provide context.
- Different benchmarks represent different market segments.
- An appropriate benchmark should represent the type of investments being
  evaluated.

RELATED TERMS

performance, relative return, portfolio, S&P 500

================================================================================
16. INDEX VS. STOCK EXCHANGE
================================================================================

DEFINITION

An index measures the performance of a selected group of securities.

A stock exchange is a marketplace where securities are listed and traded.

EXAMPLE

Nasdaq is a stock exchange.

The Nasdaq Composite is an index.

NYSE is a stock exchange.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average and S&P 500 are indexes.

SIMPLE EXPLANATION

Exchange = where securities are traded.

Index = a measurement of how a selected group of securities is performing.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"The S&P 500 is a stock exchange."

No. The S&P 500 is an index.

RELATED TERMS

NYSE, Nasdaq, S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite, stock exchange

================================================================================
17. INDEX VS. ETF
================================================================================

DEFINITION

An index is a measurement.

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is an investment fund whose shares can be bought
and sold.

Some ETFs are designed to track the performance of an index.

KEY POINTS

- An index defines or measures a basket of securities.
- An ETF is an actual investment product.
- An index does not itself have shares that investors purchase.
- An index-tracking ETF attempts to provide performance corresponding to its
  target index, subject to the fund's expenses and tracking differences.

EXAMPLE

The S&P 500 is an index.

An ETF may be designed to track the S&P 500.

Buying shares of that ETF is not the same thing as buying the index itself.

RELATED TERMS

ETF, index fund, benchmark, tracking error, expense ratio

================================================================================
18. WHY CAN'T YOU INVEST DIRECTLY IN AN INDEX?
================================================================================

An index is a mathematical measurement rather than an investment security.

It does not issue shares that investors can purchase.

Instead, investors who want exposure to the securities represented by an
index commonly use investment products designed to track that index, such as
index mutual funds or exchange-traded funds.

SIMPLE EXAMPLE

Think of an index like a scoreboard.

The scoreboard measures what is happening, but you cannot purchase the
scoreboard's score.

An index fund or ETF is an investment product that attempts to follow the
index.

SAMPLE Q&A

Q: Can I buy the S&P 500?

A: You cannot purchase the S&P 500 index itself because an index is a
measurement, not an investment security. Investors can instead purchase shares
of funds designed to track the S&P 500.

Q: Is an ETF the same thing as an index?

A: No. An index measures a collection of securities. An ETF is an investment
fund whose shares can be traded. Some ETFs are designed to track an index.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
================================================================================

"The Dow and S&P 500 are the same thing."
No. They contain different companies and use different weighting methodologies.

"The S&P 500 contains the 500 most expensive stocks."
No. Stock price alone does not determine S&P 500 membership or weighting.

"The Nasdaq is an index."
Nasdaq can refer to the stock exchange. The Nasdaq Composite and Nasdaq-100 are
indexes associated with Nasdaq-listed securities.

"If the Dow rises, every U.S. stock rose."
No. The Dow measures only its constituents.

"I can buy the S&P 500."
Not directly. An index is a measurement. Investment funds can be designed to
track it.

"A 20% weight means 20% of the companies in the index."
No. Weight describes how much influence an investment has in the index, not the
percentage of constituent companies.
