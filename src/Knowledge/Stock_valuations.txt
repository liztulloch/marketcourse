STOCK VALUATION: TERM DEFINITIONS

Earnings per share (EPS)
Net income allocated to each outstanding share of common stock. Formula: EPS = (Net Income - Preferred Dividends) / Weighted Average Shares Outstanding. Basic EPS uses shares outstanding; diluted EPS also counts shares that could be created by options, warrants, and convertible securities. Used as the denominator in P/E.

Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)
Share price relative to earnings per share. Shows how much investors pay for each dollar of earnings. Formula: P/E = Share Price / EPS (or Market Cap / Net Income). Example: price $100, EPS $5 = P/E of 20. Not meaningful when earnings are negative or near zero. Typical levels vary by industry and growth rate.

Forward vs. trailing P/E
Trailing P/E uses EPS from the last 12 months of reported results (actual, backward-looking). Forward P/E uses estimated EPS for the next 12 months or next fiscal year, usually analyst consensus (forecast, forward-looking). Forward P/E depends on estimates, which can be wrong or revised. For a growing company, forward P/E is typically lower than trailing P/E.

Price-to-sales ratio (P/S)
Market value relative to revenue. Formula: P/S = Market Cap / Total Revenue (or Share Price / Revenue per Share). Useful for companies with no profits, since revenue is positive even when earnings are not. Ignores profitability and debt, so a low P/S does not guarantee a bargain.

Price-to-book ratio (P/B)
Market value relative to book value of equity. Formula: P/B = Share Price / Book Value per Share (or Market Cap / Shareholders' Equity). A P/B below 1 means the stock trades below the accounting value of its net assets. Most relevant for asset-heavy businesses such as banks and insurers. Less useful for companies whose value is mostly intangible (software, brands).

PEG ratio
P/E adjusted for earnings growth. Formula: PEG = P/E / Expected Annual EPS Growth Rate (in percent). Example: P/E of 30 with 15% expected growth = PEG of 2. A PEG near or below 1 is commonly read as fairly priced or cheap relative to growth. Depends on growth estimates, which are uncertain.

Enterprise value (EV)
Total value of a company's operations, including both equity and debt holders' claims. Formula: EV = Market Cap + Total Debt + Preferred Stock + Minority Interest - Cash and Cash Equivalents. Represents the approximate price to buy the whole business. Capital-structure neutral, so it is better than market cap for comparing companies with different debt levels.

EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value relative to EBITDA. Formula: EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA. Ignores differences in debt, taxes, and depreciation policy, which makes it useful for comparing companies in the same industry. Lower values suggest a cheaper valuation relative to operating earnings. Less useful for businesses with heavy capital expenditures, since EBITDA excludes CapEx.

EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. A proxy for operating profitability before financing, tax, and non-cash charges. Formula: EBITDA = Net Income + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + Amortization (or Operating Income + D&A). Not a GAAP measure. It is not cash flow, because it ignores CapEx, working capital changes, and interest and tax payments.

Free-cash-flow yield
Free cash flow generated per dollar of market value. Formula: FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow / Market Cap (or FCF per Share / Share Price). Can also be calculated as FCF / Enterprise Value. A higher yield suggests more cash generation relative to price. It is the inverse of a price-to-free-cash-flow ratio.

Dividend payout ratio
Share of net income paid to shareholders as dividends. Formula: Payout Ratio = Dividends / Net Income (or Dividends per Share / EPS). A low ratio means the company retains most earnings for reinvestment; a ratio above 100% means it is paying out more than it earns, which may be unsustainable. Can also be computed using free cash flow in the denominator.

Book value
The accounting value of a company's equity: total assets minus total liabilities. Formula: Book Value = Total Assets - Total Liabilities (equals shareholders' equity). Book value per share = Book Value / Shares Outstanding. Reflects historical cost accounting, so it can differ greatly from market value, especially for companies with significant intangible assets.

Intrinsic value
An estimate of what a stock is fundamentally worth based on the business's expected cash flows, growth, and risk, independent of its current market price. It is an estimate, not a fact: different analysts using different assumptions arrive at different intrinsic values. Common methods include DCF and dividend discount models.

Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that estimates a company's value as the present value of its future cash flows. Steps: (1) forecast free cash flows for a period (often 5-10 years); (2) estimate a terminal value for cash flows beyond the forecast; (3) discount all of them back to today using a discount rate (often WACC, the weighted average cost of capital). Formula: Value = sum of FCF_t / (1 + r)^t + Terminal Value / (1 + r)^N. Highly sensitive to the growth and discount rate assumptions. Dividing the resulting enterprise value after adjusting for net debt gives equity value; dividing by shares gives value per share.

Relative valuation / comparable companies
Valuing a stock by comparing its multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/S, etc.) to those of similar companies (peers in the same industry, with similar size and growth) or to its own historical average. If a stock trades at a lower multiple than comparable peers, it may be undervalued relative to them. Limitation: if the whole peer group is mispriced, the comparison inherits that error. Also called "comps."

Growth stock vs. value stock
Growth stock: a company expected to grow revenue and earnings faster than the market average. Typically has high valuation multiples (high P/E, P/S), reinvests profits rather than paying dividends, and carries more risk if growth slows.
Value stock: a company trading at a low price relative to fundamentals (low P/E, low P/B), often mature and stable, and frequently paying dividends. Investors buy expecting the market to eventually price it higher.
The categories are not mutually exclusive and the line between them is blurry; classification depends on the metrics used.

What "overvalued" and "undervalued" mean
Overvalued: the market price is higher than an estimate of the stock's intrinsic value (or higher than comparable benchmarks justify). Undervalued: the market price is lower than that estimate.
Both are judgments relative to a chosen valuation model and its assumptions, not objective facts. They do not predict short-term price moves: an overvalued stock can keep rising and an undervalued stock can keep falling for long periods. Claims of over- or undervaluation are only as reliable as the inputs (growth, discount rate, peer choice) behind them.
